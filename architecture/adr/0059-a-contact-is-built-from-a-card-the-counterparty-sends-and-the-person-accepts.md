# A contact is built from a card the counterparty sends over the chat and the person accepts, bound to the PeerId it arrived on

**Status:** Accepted — the owner's direction of 2026-10-21 ("create a contact system, internal, absolutely separated from the Android contact system … load its own contact data, then send it to a counterparty by app with some tag … associated to the id from which the contact details are received"); the bounds and the acceptance rule in it are architect-cto's reading of that direction

## Context

The human client shows a counterparty as the short PeerId. `human-client-ui.md` §4 says the title is the short PeerId "or the contact's name, once contacts exist", and the store has carried `contacts` and `contact_routes` since Stage 14's schema v5 (STATE.md) with nothing writing them: the contacts UX was carried out of Stage 15 (plan §18, carry 11) and no stage picked it up. What a contact IS was decided long ago — application metadata above transport (ADR-0032), grouping several device routes under one person without any route inheriting another's trust (ADR-0043), never a label that promotes an EndpointId or a display name to authenticated identity. What was never designed is how the name gets there: today it could only be typed by the receiver.

The owner's direction fills that gap: each person loads their own contact data — name, surname, a photo — into their own client, sends it to a counterparty through the application, and the receiving client associates the data with the identifier it was received from. Three things in this repository constrain the shape. The chat envelope already has a `kind` field (HUMAN-CHAT.md), today with the single value `text`, and its parser rejects any other kind as malformed — so a new kind is a contract amendment, not a new protocol. The transport payload ceiling is 48 KiB after compression (`contracts/TRANSPORT.md`, `max_payload_bytes` ≤ 49152), which bounds any photo. And `human-client-ui.md` §3 already says labels, avatars and notes are not authenticated by transport: a card is the sender's claim about themself, and a client that wrote it into `contacts` unasked would let any peer in the trust set rename itself "Alice".

## Decision

1. **A contact card is a HumanChatV2 envelope of kind `contact_card`**, sent over the direct protocol like any text message, carrying the sender's own `name`, an optional `surname` and an optional small `photo`. It names nobody but the sender: the card has no identifier field, because the identity it is bound to is the authenticated `source_peer` of the direct message it arrived in, and the peer-asserted `source_endpoint` beside it is the route (ADR-0030, ADR-0032) — and, since ADR-0061 (A 2026-10-21 (ii)), the **user identity** the card presents: one node holds several, each an EndpointId with its own card, so a card names the identity of the endpoint it left from. The exact fields, bounds and malformed cases are HUMAN-CHAT.md's (§Contact card); the record decides that the card exists, what it may claim and how it is treated.

2. **A card is sent only on the person's explicit action** — "share a card" with a conversation's counterparty, choosing which of their cards to send — never automatically on first contact, on connection, on a card received, or to a channel. A card is never broadcast: a `contact_card` received over broadcast is ignored. Sending a card is telling one peer who one is; a client that did it unasked would publish the person's name to every peer the trust set admits. **A person keeps one card per user identity** (A 2026-10-21 (ii), ADR-0061; the owner's word of the same day, "a user should define more than one identity and choose what to send", read as identities, each an EndpointId with its card): the card is the identity's presentation — a name, an optional surname, an optional photo — kept in STATE.md's `own_cards` keyed by the identity's EndpointId, content the person wrote about themself, outside ADR-0044's three states. The conversation's identity decides which card Share sends: a conversation speaks as one identity (ADR-0061 decision 3) and shares that identity's card and no other, so the chooser of the first reading is gone; the conversation's details show that the card was shared and when, and nothing a card carries, and nothing the client sends, reveals the person's other identities. A counterparty who holds two cards from one node holds two contacts that share a PeerId, and is shown that they do (ADR-0061 decisions 4 and 5).

3. **A received card is a proposal, not a contact.** The receiving client holds it as inbound content of that conversation under ADR-0044's retention rules — unread until the person acts — and shows it as "this peer says they are <name>", beside the PeerId the trust indicators are tied to. Nothing is written to `contacts` or `contact_routes` until the person **accepts** it; accepting creates the contact — or, for a route already under a contact, updates that contact; a route of a PeerId that already has another route under a contact is another identity of that node and makes a contact of its own, the proposal saying which contact shares its node (A 2026-10-21 (ii), ADR-0061 decision 4, which withdrew the morning's one-contact-per-PeerId rule) — with the card's name and photo — `display_name` takes the name, then a space and the surname when the card carries one (the owner's word of 2026-10-21: no column for it), and the person may edit the result like any contact name — and the route `{source_peer, source_endpoint}`; declining or dismissing discards the card. **A proposal follows the retention rules of the conversation it arrived in (ADR-0044, RETENTION.md):** it is unread content until shown, and once shown and not accepted it is read-but-unkept content, gone when the process exits like any other item the person did not keep; the counterparty can send the card again. **A proposal has no Keep** (A 2026-10-21): Accept is the only way a card outlives the session, into `contacts`, and an unaccepted proposal cannot be kept as content — RETENTION.md says so beside its Keep rule. An accepted contact is application metadata outside any conversation: it stays when the conversation empties and disappears at close, and the person removes it only by deleting the contact. A later card from the same route is a new proposal that replaces nothing until accepted. The person may edit any accepted value, and a local edit is never overwritten by a later card.

4. **Grouping stays manual and trust stays per route** (ADR-0043). Two cards with the same name from two PeerIds are two proposals; the person may put both routes under one contact — one person's devices, ADR-0043 — and the client never merges on the strength of a name or a photo. Two routes of one PeerId are two user identities of one node (ADR-0061): two contacts, each holding its one route, the contact page naming the node they share; a contact holds at most one route per PeerId, and Move moves one route (A 2026-10-21 (ii): the morning's rule that moving a route moved every route of its PeerId together, and that a PeerId never ends under two contacts, is withdrawn with ADR-0061). Accepting a card changes no trust: the route was already in the data-plane trust set (or the card would not have been received), and a contact name grants nothing — ADR-0032's rule that a label is never an authentication factor is unchanged.

5. **The photo is bounded and inert until accepted.** HUMAN-CHAT.md bounds its encoded size and pixel dimensions so a card fits the payload ceiling with room for the envelope; a client decodes it with a memory-safe decoder, only after the person accepts the card, at a fixed display size, and never fetches anything: the photo is bytes in the envelope, not a reference. Before acceptance the proposal shows the name and a neutral placeholder. **The photo carries pixels and nothing else** (A 2026-10-21): the sending client re-encodes it and strips every metadata block — EXIF, XMP, GPS, embedded thumbnails, the file name — before it enters the card, so a position or a camera serial is never a disclosure the person did not choose; a receiver treats any metadata that arrives as absent and shows none of it.

6. **The contact system is the application's own and touches no platform address book.** On Android the client declares no contacts permission (`READ_CONTACTS`, `WRITE_CONTACTS`, `GET_ACCOUNTS`), uses no contacts provider or picker, and exports nothing to one; on desktop it reads no system address book. The store's `contacts` and `contact_routes` tables are the only contact storage, at rest under ADR-0060. A tree check over the Android manifest and the Gradle dependency list pins the first half (owed with the implementation, CLAUDE.md §4's rule on a "never").

7. **Desktop and Android share the implementation**: the `chat-protocol`, `store`, `app-core` and `ui-model` crates are the ones both clients already build on (ADR-0039), so the card, the proposal, the acceptance and the contact rows are written once; each graphical client adds its screens and the command-line client (decision 9) its commands. An automated client (ADR-0055) receives a card as data like any payload, may surface the name to its operator, and routes and trusts on the PeerId alone, as before.

8. **The recovery phrase restores no contact** (ADR-0043, `human-client-ui.md` §9): a card accepted on one device lives in that device's store. No backup carries a contact today: ADR-0044's future backup covers message content only, and the device-to-device backup over InterWeave of ADR-0060 decision 8 carries contacts only if its own record says so. A person who wants the contact on a second device accepts the counterparty's card there.

9. **Every action of this record has a command-line form.** The owner's word of 2026-10-21 ("a cli way to do everything must exist"): InterWeave is the transport of the fleet's control plane and an automated client is a first-party consumer (ADR-0055), so keeping and editing one's own cards, sharing one to a conversation, listing proposals, accepting or declining one, listing, editing and deleting contacts and their routes, and the store actions of ADR-0060 (health, discard and recreate) are each a command of a command-line human client built on the same shared crates as the two graphical ones, usable with no display and scriptable from a shell; the two graphical clients add nothing a command cannot do. `transportctl` stays the daemon's administrative tool and gains none of this, because contacts are application state above the transport (ADR-0032). The client's name, its argument shape and its output format are the implementer's, recorded in `architecture/clients/human/` when it lands.

## Alternatives considered

- **The receiver types the name** (the state until now). Rejected as the only path: it is what makes every conversation a PeerId until the person does clerical work, and the typed name is no more authenticated than a card. It stays as the edit path of decision 3.
- **Write the card straight into `contacts` on receipt.** Rejected: any peer in the trust set could name itself, and rename itself later, with nothing but a message. Acceptance is the one step that keeps a contact the person's own record.
- **A card that names a third party** (an introduction, "this is Bob at PeerId X"). Rejected for v1: it is a social graph the transport does not define (`human-client-model-b.md`) and it would hand a peer a way to pre-label a stranger. Revisit.
- **Use the platform's contact system** (Android contacts provider, a desktop address book) as the store or the source. Rejected on the owner's word and on privacy: it would require a contacts permission, mix application contacts with the person's whole address book, and sync them through whatever the platform syncs.
- **A separate protocol** (`/interweave/contact/1.0.0`) instead of a chat kind. Rejected: it would be a new transport protocol for an application payload, against ADR-0032's boundary; the chat envelope's `kind` field exists for exactly this.
- **A photo by reference** (a URL) to escape the ceiling. Rejected: HUMAN-CHAT.md already refuses automatic fetches because they leak IP, presence and read timing; a card's photo would be fetched on every render.
- **Automatic exchange on first message**, so the names appear without any action. Rejected: it publishes the person's name to every peer in the trust set and makes a connection a disclosure. The action stays explicit; the UI may offer it when a conversation is first opened.

## Consequences

Conversations gain names the counterparty chose, with one tap by the receiver and none of the keyboard work the Stage 15 carry assumed. The chat contract gains a second kind, and the parser's "not `text` is malformed" rule becomes "not a known kind is malformed", unchanged in effect for every client built so far since nothing has shipped. The store's two dormant tables get their writers. The carried contacts UX (plan §18, carry 11) is this record's work, and decisions 2, 3 and 6 are input to the owner's privacy review named there, not its discharge: the owner answers that review at §29's exit gate (d), over the card and over what STATE.md already puts in it (`read_pairs`, `conversation_index`, `contact_routes.last_seen`).

## Security implications

A card is peer-controlled content and is treated as such end to end: it is held as inbound content under the retention rules, rendered through the same provenance framing as text (HUMAN-CHAT.md §Consumers), and becomes part of the person's records only on their acceptance. The impersonation move — a trusted peer naming itself as someone the person knows — is not prevented by this record (no application identity system can, without a stronger binding ADR-0032 leaves to a future layer); it is made visible: the proposal always shows the PeerId the trust indicators are tied to, and a second contact with an existing name is shown as such before acceptance. The photo is the one parser-facing input the card adds; it is bounded on the wire, decoded only after acceptance and only by a memory-safe decoder, so an undecodable or hostile image costs one rejected card. Sending a card is a disclosure of the person's name and face to one peer, on their action; decision 2 keeps it from becoming a disclosure to the whole trust set. Several cards of one's own (decision 2) are pseudonyms only toward peers who never compare: every one of them arrives bound to the same PeerId, any two holders who compare link them at once, and a person who needs identities that cannot be linked needs separate profiles, a separate record's subject. No contacts permission means the application cannot read or leak the device's address book, by construction rather than by policy.

## Operational implications

Nothing changes for the daemon or the transport: a card is a direct message under the 49,152-byte hard ceiling, and under a profile's lower configured limit the client offers to send it without the photo (HUMAN-CHAT.md §Contact card). The Android manifest gains nothing; the tree check of decision 6 fails a change that adds a contacts permission or a contacts library. Stage 19's upgrade and rollback rows learn the migration that adds the contacts' provenance columns (A 2026-10-21), and any schema change that holds a card proposal, if the implementation needs one beyond the `unread_inbound` row the envelope already lands in.

## Implementation implications

HUMAN-CHAT.md §Contact card is the wire shape and the malformed cases, with fixture cases added under its §Fixture cases (a valid card, a card over the bounds, a card with an unknown photo type, a card with a third-party field). `human-client-ui.md` §3 carries the proposal, acceptance and display rules. The store's `contacts.display_name` and `avatar_ref` take the accepted values, and `name_source`, `name_at`, `photo_source` and `photo_at` record where each came from and when (A 2026-10-21, the migration plan §29 step 2 lands); whether a proposal lives in `unread_inbound` (the envelope as received, which the retention rules already govern) or in a row of its own is the implementer's, recorded in STATE.md. The code is rust-ui-dev's, in the shared crates and the two clients; the acceptance tests are theirs under `human-client-ui.md` §13, with the conformance cases named in plan §29. This record opens no stage: §29 names the work and the owner opens it.

## Revisit conditions

When an application identity layer with a stronger binding than the PeerId exists (ADR-0032's "higher-level application identity system"), when a third-party introduction is wanted, when the device-to-device backup of ADR-0060 decision 8 is designed and should carry contacts, or when a client is asked to show a card's photo before acceptance.

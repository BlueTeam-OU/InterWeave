# Automated clients are first-party consumers above a general-purpose transport: durability, receipts, fan-out and framing live in the client, and the fleet's own agents are the first deployment

**Status:** Accepted (2026-10-19; the owner's word "Arm #305" of 2026-10-19, given by the arming of the pull request that landed this record, under the ratification-by-merge rule of 2026-09-27; the status flipped in the series that closes Stage 20, plan §28, which names the merge)

## Context

The owner directed on 2026-10-19 that the agents that build InterWeave use it as their transport, and the same day drew the line that this record keeps: InterWeave remains a general-purpose transport, available for human-to-human communication as for anything else, and the agents' coordination protocol does not go into this repository — "different things, different layer". So this record decides what the transport is to an automated client in general, and the fleet's own agents are its first deployment, recorded on the fabric's side (agent-fabric ADR-064, the one-host dual run accepted 2026-10-23, and the planned coordination repository; the citation read ADR-062 until A 2026-10-23, a record on another subject) with everything that is theirs: the protocol, its addressing, its roster of who a login is, its journal and its carrier process. No name, rule, test or document of that protocol enters this repository.

What InterWeave already is, read from `main` (441361da): a profile-scoped daemon with a PeerId per profile (ADR-0015, ADR-0016); directed delivery to one endpoint under a PeerId with `AcceptedV2` as bounded queue admission and a coarse `no_route` (ADR-0005, ADR-0030, ADR-0018); signed GossipSub broadcast whose publisher is the authenticated PeerId (ADR-0004, ADR-0029); a deny-by-default trust allowlist with a persisted overlay (ADR-0012, ADR-0028); IPC v2 over a per-uid socket with an exclusive endpoint lease, a 48 KiB payload and a 128 KiB frame (ADR-0017, ADR-0026, ADR-0037); static bootstrap whose operator-supplied addresses are admitted whatever their class, loopback included (ADR-0052 rule 9); a transport that is realtime and non-durable by decision (ADR-0018, ADR-0020, ADR-0044). What it was not, before this record: a document that names an automated, non-human client as a first-party consumer; a command that mints or shows an identity without the daemon; a service unit; a test that dials a loopback static-bootstrap entry; and three measured facts an automated client needs (decision 2).

## Decision

1. **An automated client is a first-party consumer**, with the same standing as the human client and the Claude Code Channel bridge: one long-lived process per profile, running under the profile's account, holding the data-socket lease of one configured endpoint under one client kind the deployment chooses, negotiating keepalive, reconnecting to its daemon with bounded backoff, a fresh lease and fresh joins when the daemon goes away and comes back (the bridge's shape, ADR-0002 A 2026-10-07), and draining its event queue as part of holding the lease. It never opens the admin socket; trust changes stay with `transportctl` under the account's own hand.
2. **What the transport promises such a client, stated so that no client assumes more.** `AcceptedV2` is queue admission at the receiver, never delivery (ADR-0044). `no_route` is coarse (ADR-0005). There is no durable broadcast and no offline mailbox (ADR-0020). Duplicate suppression is bounded and in memory by decision (ADR-0019: 10,000 entries, five minutes, persistence prohibited), so a retry under one `message_id` lands once only within the receiver's window and uptime, and twice after the window or a receiving daemon's restart. The per-client queue holds 256 events: a direct message that finds it full is refused `overloaded` before `AcceptedV2`, a broadcast drops the oldest, and a client that does not drain is closed as wedged and loses its lease (LOCAL-IPC.md). The receiver's broadcast ingress bucket — 32 in a burst, 120 a minute per peer — drops what exceeds it before local delivery with no refusal to the sender, no log line and no counter (measured 2026-10-19 over thirteen runs: 34–41 of 200 delivered, each run inside the bound the bucket's constants give over its elapsed time), while the same burst as direct sends lands whole through `Overloaded` refusals the sender sees and retries (200 of 200; the refusal count is a timing-dependent sample, 632–686 over thirteen runs and 568 in a later one, the duration about 84 s from 168 refills at two a second: 32 at once, then two a second). **Broadcast order is not preserved**: in one of those runs the delivered messages were out of the publisher's order, so no client assumes it; direct sends from one sender, sent one at a time and retried in turn, arrived in order in every run, and nothing promises that either. The payload ceiling is 48 KiB and does not rise for any client (ADR-0026). A clean daemon stop answers `ShuttingDown` on the session and every later call, where a vanished daemon answers `BackendUnavailable`: both mean the session is gone and is re-opened.
3. **What a client that needs more owes itself, above the transport**, where every client states its own guarantee as ADR-0044 made the human client do: its own durable pending record with one `message_id` kept per message for retry, and a retry that is never given up on without its operator; its own application-level receipt where it needs delivery rather than admission, sent by the receiving client after it has secured the message, opaque to the transport (ADR-0001); its own collapse of a repeated message by its own identity, since the transport's window is bounded; fan-out as one direct send per recipient when every recipient must receive, since a publish reaches only the subscribed and the bucket drops silently; compression and chunking under its own framing when its messages exceed the ceiling (`;ce=br` is the content-encoding the human facade already defines); and its own authentication of who a PeerId is, since the transport authenticates the PeerId and nothing above it (ADR-0012: a PeerId is not a person, a role or a principal). Moving any of this into the runtime stays rejected (ADR-0044 §Alternatives).
4. **Provisioning without a running daemon.** `transportctl identity init --profile <name>` mints a key with no daemon and prints the PeerId; `identity show` prints it for an existing key; both take the profile lock as the other identity verbs do. A profile's `config.yaml` is rendered by the deployment from its own record of who is who, never hand-edited on the host, and a trust change reaches a running daemon through `transportctl trust allow|revoke` (the persisted overlay) until the next render.
5. **A one-host deployment of many profiles under many accounts**, the fleet's shape: each profile listens on `127.0.0.1` with a port the deployment fixes per profile, every other profile's PeerId in its allowlist and its loopback address a static-bootstrap entry (operator-supplied, admitted under ADR-0052 rule 9). A daemon whose port is held by a socket without `SO_REUSEPORT` exits 1 on `EADDRINUSE` (a case of `tests/automated-client`) and its unit retries it (the unit measured on the host's user manager on 2026-10-19: four retries in 23 s, under the start limit — a measurement, not a case); within one account two daemons share a port silently, since the TCP transport listens with `SO_REUSEPORT` (measured: two `LISTEN` sockets on one port in the kernel's table), so a deployment runs one profile per account on one port and never two. That another account's daemon is refused the port this one holds rests on the kernel's documented `SO_REUSEPORT` rule (same effective uid) and was measured on the host on 2026-10-27 between two accounts, each under its own user manager with the installed v0.1.0 programs and `DEPLOYING.md`'s unit and configuration: the second account's daemon exited 1 on the held port and its unit retried every 5 s, and once the holder stopped, the next retry listened (`packaging/linux/evidence/2026-10-27-second-uid-port.md`; A 2026-10-27); the suite itself still has one uid, and says so, so a second account in CI would make it a case rather than a host record. No connectivity behaviour is needed: the server roles stay off and the client roles every daemon composes have no static servers to use; ADR-0034 §7's release gate stays where it is; decision 5b adds a second host over a private overlay the deployment provides and consumes neither it nor SPIKE-004's deferred limits, which wait for a host reached across the open Internet (A 2026-10-27 (ii)).
   5b. **A two-host deployment over a private overlay the deployment provides** (A 2026-10-27, the owner's direction that the fabric's control plane rides InterWeave through the command-line client so an agent can run on a remote host): each daemon listens on a wildcard address rather than `127.0.0.1` — never on the overlay literal alone, since a listener bound to one address reports no change when its link goes, so the lost overlay is no network change and the connection neither closes nor is redialled until something else times out, while a wildcard listener's bound set loses the address and `CONNECTIVITY.md` §14's detector closes and redials within seconds (measured 2026-10-27: `a_listener_on_the_overlay_literal_keeps_its_connection_past_the_close_window` in `tests/automated-client/tests/two_hosts.rs` keeps the overlay-literal listeners' connection open past the 10 s close window within which the wildcard case closes; the same reason an embedded-android profile's listeners are wildcard-only) — and its peer's overlay address with its PeerId is a static-bootstrap entry — operator-supplied, admitted under ADR-0052 rule 9's door whatever its class, since the operator door sits outside rule 2's class floor, so a carrier-grade, RFC 1918 or ULA literal needs nothing of the transport. The overlay — its tunnel, its addressing, its key exchange — is the deployment's to provide and is not InterWeave's; no Kademlia and no connectivity role is turned on, as decision 5 says, and ADR-0034 §7's release gate and SPIKE-004's deferred limits are unchanged by it. What proves the shape is a case of `tests/automated-client` with two daemons in two network namespaces on one runner, joined by a point-to-point link on a private prefix — each with its own interfaces, addresses, routing table, port space and network-change detector, no loopback between them — checking a direct message each way and a broadcast over the static entries, the link going down and coming back (the connection closed and redialled), and a positive control, the same profiles without the static entry never connecting. The known limits that case leaves, by name: one kernel, so no real latency, loss, MTU or clock skew from a tunnel and no real overlay in the path; no NAT; no second uid in the case itself (decision 5's port rule is measured on the host, A 2026-10-27, and still owed as a CI case); a real second host needs a runner on a second machine or the hosted edge itself as a test peer, and is not run in CI. Whether those limits are accepted for the first remote host is the owner's word, recorded in this record's history when given.
6. **Linux service units ship with the transport**: a daemon unit templated on the profile name, and a client unit template that `After=` and `BindsTo=` its daemon, both systemd user units running the account's own binaries with no system-wide install — Stage 19's service-lifecycle rows pulled forward for Linux only.
7. **The fleet is the first deployment, and the line is the socket.** InterWeave ends at the data socket's contract and the facts above; the fabric's record holds the protocol's mapping onto endpoints and sends, its roster, its journal, its receipt and its carrier process, and nothing of it is cited here by name. A human client and a machine client are peers on the same transport under the same rules, which is what "general purpose" means for this record.

## Alternatives considered

A spool or receipt inside `TransportRuntime` (rejected by ADR-0044 and again here: the guarantee stays realtime so that every client states its own). A protocol-specific carrier in this repository (refused by the owner: the protocol is a different layer, and the transport must serve human-to-human use unchanged). Relying on broadcast for a client that needs every message delivered (the bucket drops silently; measured). Raising the payload ceiling for one client class (it is every peer's and the frame's). Letting two profiles of one account share a port (silent with `SO_REUSEPORT`). Hand-written `config.yaml` per profile on a host of many (they drift; the deployment renders them).

## Consequences

Any automated client — an agent, a bot, a gateway, a test harness — reads one document for what it gets and what it owes; the human client's rules are untouched and nothing here narrows them. The fleet's deployment is recorded on the fabric's side with its own figures; this record gains a `Met.` sentence citing the first deployment only as a fact. The transport gains two offline verbs, two unit templates, one document and one conformance suite, and no protocol.

## Security implications

The transport authenticates the PeerId (Noise for direct, signed GossipSub for broadcast) and nothing above it: who a PeerId is, is the deployment's record and the client's check, never a claim on the wire. The per-uid socket keeps one account's client out of another's daemon. The silent broadcast drop is a measured availability fact, not a security boundary, and a client that needs delivery does not rely on broadcast. Not claimed: Byzantine resistance among profiles, protection from same-uid code, any property of a second host.

## Operational implications

Two user units per profile on a host of many accounts; a port allocation held in one place by the deployment; a runbook that reads the sender's pending record first and the receiver's daemon second; the ingress bucket's figures in the automated-client document so that a sender paces fan-out (2 a second sustained to one peer after the first 32).

## Implementation implications

In this repository, a stage that ran beside Stage 17 by record and closed on it the same day (plan §28): the two `transportctl` verbs (landed on p2p-network-dev's branch at 8dddbbcf, five tests beside the file's nineteen for the earlier verbs), the Linux unit templates, `architecture/clients/automated/AUTOMATED-CLIENT.md` carrying decision 2 and 3 with the test or contract that holds each fact, and `tests/automated-client` proving them against two daemons on loopback with a generic test client. In the fabric, by its own records, everything the protocol needs above the socket.

## Revisit conditions

A second host reached across the open Internet (ADR-0034 §7 cleared, SPIKE-004's limits consumed; decision 5b's private overlay is not one); the broadcast ingress bucket gaining a counter or a refusal (then decision 2's sentence is updated, not the client's rule); the Channel bridge gaining a standalone mode; the ceiling proving too small for a client class (then the client splits, never the ceiling rises).

# A person holds several network identities: each is a whole profile, one client attaches to several, and every conversation speaks as exactly one of them

**Status:** Accepted — the owner's direction of 2026-10-21 ("Also important, a user should have multiple network identities"); the shape — an identity is a profile and nothing less, the client attaches to several, a conversation belongs to one — and the bounds in it are architect-cto's reading of that direction

## Context

What an identity is here was settled early and has held: one persistent network identity per named transport profile, never one per conversation and never one hidden per client (ADR-0016); each concurrently active device or profile its own PeerId, the 24-word phrase restoring exactly one of them (ADR-0033, ADR-0043); a profile's seed sealed per profile on Android (ADR-0042, the Keystore alias carries the profile name); a profile's human store under its own state directory (STATE.md, `<state_dir>/human`, and ADR-0060's key beside it); a profile's trust set its own (ADR-0012, ADR-0028's overlay). A host may run as many profiles as it has daemons (ADR-0016, ADR-0055's one-host deployment of many profiles under many accounts). What the clients do with that today is narrower than the model: the desktop human client takes one `--profile <name>` and speaks to that one daemon for its whole life (`apps/human-desktop`), and the Android app resolves one constant profile name in its one embedded runtime (`apps/human-android`, `ProfilePaths::resolve_embedded(PROFILE, …)`). A person who wants a second identity today runs a second desktop client against a second daemon, and on Android cannot.

ADR-0059 made the gap visible from the other side: several cards of one's own are presentation under one PeerId, and its security section says in as many words that a person who needs identities that cannot be linked needs separate profiles. The owner's direction is that such a person is the normal case, not the exception: a user holds several network identities and chooses which one to use.

## Decision

1. **An identity is a profile, and nothing less.** Every network identity a person holds is a transport profile as ADR-0016 defines it: its own Ed25519 seed and PeerId, its own recovery phrase (ADR-0033), its own trust set and endpoint configuration, its own key custody (the seed's and the store key's, ADR-0042 and ADR-0060), its own human store with its own contacts, conversations and own cards (ADR-0059), its own listeners and its own daemon or embedded runtime. Nothing of one identity is shared with another by the transport or by the application: no common EndpointId, no common relay reservation, no common directory entry, no table in one store naming another identity. The one record that names several identities is the client's own list of the profiles it presents — profile names, nothing else, no PeerId, no content, the equivalent of a launcher's list — kept outside every profile's store and never sent. The transport is unchanged by this record; what changes is how many profiles one client holds.

2. **One client attaches to several identities.** The desktop client attaches to every profile the person has placed under it — the profile's configuration carries which client kinds it admits already; it gains the person's choice that this client presents it — each through that profile's own daemon and data socket, as separate IPC connections and separate slots (ADR-0032, LOCAL-CLIENT.md), never one connection for two profiles; two profiles configured on one fixed listen port are refused at attach, by name, since two daemons under one account would otherwise share the port silently (ADR-0055 decision 5). The Android app hosts one embedded runtime per **active** identity inside its one foreground service (ADR-0041): each with its own listeners, its own network-change handling and its own custody, none sharing a port or a socket. An identity is **active** (its runtime running, the identity reachable) or **dormant** (configured, nothing running, unreachable); the person sets that per identity, and the number active at once on a phone is bounded — **four** by this record, a configuration value the implementation reads and SPIKE-012 confirms or moves with its figures. The service's one persistent notification names the active identities; ADR-0041's stay-reachable opt-in is per identity, and a change to one identity's restarts that identity's runtime alone (ADR-0041 A 2026-10-21).

3. **A conversation belongs to exactly one identity.** A direct conversation is keyed by the local identity and the remote route, `{local PeerId, remote PeerId, EndpointId?}`, a channel conversation by the local identity and the ChannelId; a conversation never changes identity, and every message, card and receipt it sends travels under that identity's PeerId. Whenever the client holds more than one identity, active or dormant, starting a conversation asks which identity speaks — a dormant one is offered as "activate and speak as this identity", subject to decision 2's bound, since a dormant identity is unreachable and can send nothing — and every conversation shows which one it speaks as, in its title's details and beside the composer, so the person never sends under an identity they did not mean; a conversation whose identity is dormant shows it as unreachable and refuses to send until the person activates it. A client holding one identity asks nothing and shows the identity only where it is asked for. A card shared in a conversation is one of that identity's own cards (ADR-0059 decision 2), and a card received is a proposal in that identity's store.

4. **Contacts are per identity and never merged by the client.** Each identity's `contacts` and `contact_routes` are its own; the client shows no cross-identity list, suggests no match between two identities' contacts, and reuses nothing — a counterparty known to identity A is a stranger to identity B until B's own trust set admits it and B's own store holds it. The one cross-identity act is explicit: the person may copy a contact's routes from one identity to another, on their action, which creates nothing in the destination's trust set.

5. **What the protocol says about two identities of one person, and what it does not hide.** No wire field, protocol name, directory response or discovery announcement carries more than one PeerId of one person, and nothing a client sends names another identity: that is the whole of what the transport promises. It does not hide the device: two identities active on one host share its IP addresses, and the protocol hands those to every counterparty — the source address of each connection, the listen and observed addresses Identify exchanges on every connection, the addresses a Kademlia record publishes — so a counterparty who talks to both identities sees the same addresses and can link them, as can an observer on the network, a relay both identities use, and anyone on the LAN that the mDNS provider (`discovery/providers/mdns.md`) announces each active identity to. Unlinkability of two identities is therefore not promised by default; a person who needs it configures the identities apart — separate relays and no direct listeners for the identity that must not be linked, an explicit opt-in configuration this record names and does not make the default — and the identity settings say both limits in plain words, the counterparty's and the observer's.

6. **Recovery is per identity.** Each identity has its own 24-word phrase and its own recovery flow (`human-client-ui.md` §9); the phrase restores that identity alone, and a device that loses its store loses that identity's contacts and conversations alone (ADR-0060). Importing an identity that is active elsewhere warns as ADR-0043 says, per identity.

7. **Every action has a command-line form** (ADR-0059 decision 9): list the identities the client holds, create one, import one from its phrase, set one active or dormant (the active count under decision 2's bound), remove one from the client (never deleting its profile without the explicit confirmed action), and name the identity on every contact and conversation command.

8. **Automated clients are unchanged**: one profile per agent (ADR-0055), with no reason to hold several; a deployment that wants two identities runs two profiles as it does today.

9. **Nothing of one identity reaches a place another identity's surfaces read.** Every piece of per-identity client state — caches, thumbnails, search indexes, drafts, crash reports' breadcrumbs, the conversation list's own pointers — lives under that identity's profile directory and is opened only while that identity is in front or sending; a notification names the identity it arrived on and carries nothing of another identity's contacts or content; the service's one persistent notification names the active identities and nothing else; a crash report or diagnostic names at most the identity it was taken under. "A place another identity reads" is any file, cache, index or screen outside that identity's profile directory, and the conformance case is a notification for identity A's contact arriving while identity B's conversation is in front: the notification names A and nothing of B is shown with it, and B's screens change nothing.

## Alternatives considered

- **One PeerId, several personas (the cards of ADR-0059 as identities).** Rejected: a persona under one PeerId is linked to the others by every counterparty who compares, and ADR-0016 and ADR-0043 already refuse hidden sub-identities under one key.
- **EndpointIds as identities.** Rejected: an EndpointId is a route under a PeerId, peer-controlled and unauthenticated as identity (ADR-0030, ADR-0032); two endpoints are one identity to the network.
- **A second installation of the app per identity** (work profiles, cloned apps). Rejected as the design: it is the person's workaround today and stays possible, but it gives no shared client, no per-conversation choice, and on Android two foreground services.
- **A hidden shared layer** (one store for all identities, one trust set with identity tags). Rejected: one table naming two identities is the link decision 1 forbids, and a bug in the tag is a cross-identity send.
- **An account or device-linking protocol above transport.** Deferred, as ADR-0043's revisit condition says; this record needs none of it.
- **No bound on active identities on a phone.** Rejected: each runtime holds sockets, listeners and a Keystore session; SPIKE-012 measures what a phone bears and the bound is read from configuration so the figure can move.

## Consequences

The desktop client becomes a client of several daemons; the Android service becomes a host of several runtimes; the conversation key, the contact model and every screen that names "the identity" gain the local PeerId as a dimension; the command-line client gains the identity verbs. A person with one identity sees no change: no chooser, and the identity shown only where it is asked for. Stage 17's packages are touched (the Android service, the onboarding flow), which is why the work is a stage of its own, §30, opened on the owner's word after §29 or beside it. ADR-0016's rationale is confirmed, not amended; ADR-0041 is amended with this record (A 2026-10-21): the service owns one `human` lease per active identity, the stay-reachable choice is per identity, and a change restarts that identity's runtime alone rather than the service, the service following the most reachable mode any active identity holds.

## Security implications

Separation is by construction, not by policy: two identities are two profiles, so a key, a store or a trust record of one cannot reach the other without crossing a profile boundary the code already enforces (separate directories, separate sockets, separate custody aliases). The new risks are in the client that holds both: a send under the wrong identity, which decision 3 answers by binding the conversation to one identity for life and showing it; and a cross-identity leak through the client's own state — a shared cache, a notification that names identity A's contact while B is in front, a crash report, a search index — which decision 9 answers: per-identity state under the identity's own profile directory, a notification naming the identity it arrived on and nothing of another's. Decision 5 states the limit honestly: the counterparty, the network observer, the shared relay and the LAN see one device, and only an opt-in configuration apart changes that. An attacker holding the device sees every identity it holds, as they see every file; the identities' custody (ADR-0042's policies, ADR-0060's keys) applies per identity and no identity's unlock opens another.

## Operational implications

A phone running several active identities keeps several runtimes awake under one notification, with the battery and memory cost SPIKE-012 measures; dormant identities cost nothing but storage. A desktop person runs one daemon per identity, as today, and one client. Recovery screens, settings and the notification all name the identity they speak of. Stage 19's packaging carries nothing new: a daemon per profile is already its shape.

## Implementation implications

SPIKE-012 (SPIKES.md, plan §3's table) runs before §30's Android step: two embedded runtimes in one foreground service on the reference device — ports, listeners, network-change handling, memory and battery over a measured window, the custody of two seeds under two alias sets, the notification — with its Result confirming or moving the bound of decision 2. The desktop half needs no spike: a second IPC connection to a second daemon is the shape the client already uses for its data and admin connections. The work is plan §30's, rust-ui-dev's in the shared crates and the two clients; `human-client-ui.md` §3 and §4 carry the per-identity rules from this record's landing; LOCAL-CLIENT.md is unchanged (one profile per connection, as before); the plan's §30 names the conformance cases, among them a conversation that cannot change identity, a card sent under the conversation's identity and no other, no cross-identity row in any store, and the bound on active identities refused above it.

## Revisit conditions

When SPIKE-012 reports its figures (the bound of decision 2); when a device-linking or account protocol above transport is designed (ADR-0043's revisit); when the owner wants a cross-identity view the person opts into; or when ADR-0041's foreground-service model changes.
